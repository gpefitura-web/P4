\begin{abstract}
Some Sun-like stars lose their activity cycle, as the Sun did in the Maunder minimum. If such a loss behaves like a critical transition, the lag-1 autocorrelation and the variance of the activity record should rise beforehand. We test this on annual means of the Mount Wilson Ca\,\textsc{ii} H and K index (S) for \NStarsRun{} stars and for a composite solar record, in segments of \LMin{} to \NSunYears{} years. Rolling-window indicators are computed for the original series and three detrended versions, and tested against \NSurr{} surrogates from autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models that reproduce the gaps and interpolation of the data. HD~166620, a candidate for entering a grand minimum, has lower S-index variability in the grand minimum than in two earlier cycle minima (H1, confirmatory, using intervals already published). No segment shows a consistent rise in the temporal indicators (H2, exploratory). The Mount Wilson record of HD~166620 ends before its transition, so H2 could not be tested as designed, and the other segments are a null scan of series with no known transition. The surrogate test has nominal size (\AutoUp\,\% and \AutoLo\,\% rejection for the upper and lower tail at a nominal 5\,\%) but little power at these lengths, and none in a simulation with a cycle. Two group differences in normalized variance survive a Holm multiple-test correction within blocks, but not over all tests; we attribute them to cycle amplitude, which we did not test.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Stars of roughly solar type show chromospheric activity that is cyclic in some, irregular in others and nearly constant in a few \cite{baliunas1995}. We follow it through the S index, the ratio of the flux in the cores of the Ca\,\textsc{ii} H and K lines to the flux in nearby continuum bands \cite{baliunas1995}. The Sun itself went through the Maunder minimum, when the cycle was strongly suppressed \cite{usoskin2017}. We ask whether a star about to lose its cycle gives a warning.

The idea comes from dynamical systems. Near a transition where a stable state disappears, recovery from small perturbations slows down, and the lag-1 autocorrelation and the variance of the time series tend to rise \cite{scheffer2009,held2004}. Rolling-window indicators of this kind, with significance tested against surrogate series, are one way to look for the effect \cite{dakos2012}. The premise needs care here. The sign of each indicator can depend on the system. A Hopf bifurcation is a critical transition only if it is subcritical \cite{kuehn2011}; the variance is analyzed on the side where the equilibrium is stable, with the covariance tending to zero for equal noise \cite{kuehn2013}; and spectral reddening need not precede transitions between oscillatory states \cite{bury2020}. None of the sources we read treats the loss of a limit cycle itself, the passage from an oscillation to a steady state, so for that direction we have no prediction of the sign. The one-sided tests below assume a rising indicator; that is an assumption, not a result.

Several public data sets make a test possible. The Mount Wilson HK Project observed dozens of stars for decades \cite{baliunas1995}, and Baum et al.\ \cite{baum2022} assembled the series of 59 stars from Mount Wilson and Keck, with instrument labels. Luhn et al.\ \cite{luhn2022} reported that HD~166620 is entering a grand minimum, which gives one star with states on both sides of a change. The Sun has its own calibrated S-index record \cite{egeland2017}.

We declared two hypotheses before computing any indicator on a real star. H1 (confirmatory): the variability of the S index of HD~166620 (a Henry Draper catalog number) in the grand minimum is smaller than in its cycle minima. H1 re-analyzes intervals and values that Luhn et al.\ already published, so it is a re-test and not a blind one. H2 (exploratory, one star): in the segment before the transition, the lag-1 autocorrelation and the variance of the detrended annual series both show a positive Kendall trend ($\tau > 0$ with one-sided $p < 0.05$ against surrogates in at least three of four detrending configurations). We also compute the same indicators in every other star with a long enough Mount Wilson segment; those results are descriptive, and the sample is too small to carry a group comparison on its own.

\section{Data and methods}

\subsection{Sample and series}

The catalog is the frozen version of the star table in Baliunas et al.\ \cite{baliunas1995} (their Table~2, \NCatalog{} stars). Its classes are Flat (S-index scatter below about 1--1.5\,\% of the mean), Cycle (a cycle period with a false-alarm class; we use the ``excellent'' ones), Var (variable without a periodic signal) and Long (variability on timescales longer than 25 years). Series come from three sources: the \NBaumStars{} stars of Baum et al.\ \cite{baum2022} (their Table~1; points flagged as outliers are removed, points flagged both as one-day and outlier are kept), the \NVTwoStars{} stars of the HK Project v2001 files \cite{radick2018}, and the calibrated solar S index of Egeland et al.\ \cite{egeland2017} (their Table~4, 1966--2016, a composite of several instruments). The \NExclCalib{} calibration stars, \NExclBinary{} binaries and the stars with no series in any source were excluded before any indicator was computed. Of the \NFlatClass{} stars that Baliunas et al.\ class as Flat, \NFlatCalib{} are calibration stars and \NFlatBin{} further ones are flagged as binaries; the other \NFlatClean{} are the clean Flat stars, and \NFlatSeries{} of them have a series. The stars analyzed are \NGroupFlat{} Flat, \NGroupCycle{} Cycle, \NGroupVar{} Var and \NGroupLong{} Long, plus the Sun, which is treated as exploratory.

For the temporal indicators each star is reduced to annual means of S, the mean of all observations in a calendar year. Only the Mount Wilson era is used, so no segment crosses the change to Keck; the change between the two Mount Wilson photometers inside that era is not corrected, and the solar composite combines five instruments as calibrated by Egeland et al. Gaps of one or two missing years are filled by linear interpolation and the filled points are marked; gaps of three or more years split the series into segments. Indicators are computed only in segments of at least \MinSeg{} years, so that a window of 50\,\% has at least 12 points and 13 windows. Segments of \MinSeg--\LowMax{} years are labeled low precision and their results exploratory; segments of \AdeqMin{} years or more are labeled adequate. Such a segment exists in \NStarsRun{} stars (\LMin--\LMax{} years) and in the Sun (\NSunYears{} years); \NNotRun{} further stars have a Mount Wilson segment shorter than \MinSeg{} years and receive a description only.

\subsection{Indicators and detrending}

In each segment we compute the lag-1 autocorrelation (AR(1) coefficient) and the variance in a sliding window, and the Kendall $\tau$ of each against window index (no tie correction; the same computation is used for the data and the surrogates). The window is a fraction $f$ of the segment length $L$, $w = \mathrm{round}(fL)$, with $f = 0.25$, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625 and 0.75; $f = 0.5$ is the default. The series is analyzed in four configurations: the original series, and the residual of a Gaussian smooth with $\sigma = 1$, 3 or 5 years (edges handled by repeating the end values). A Gaussian smooth of width $\sigma$ keeps a fraction $\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}(2\pi\sigma/P)^2]$ of the amplitude of a cycle of period $P$; the residual is thus a high-pass version of the series, not the series without its cycle. A segment is flagged for an indicator when $\tau > 0$ with $p < 0.05$ in at least three of the four configurations. H2 requires this for both indicators.

\subsection{Significance}

The $p$ value of each $\tau$ comes from \NSurr{} surrogate series from an ARMA($p,q$) model with $p,q \le 2$ chosen by the Akaike information criterion (AIC), fitted by maximum likelihood to the series (or residual) being tested after standardizing it to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (the fitted mean and variance are mapped back to the units of the series), with Gaussian innovations and a burn-in of 100 steps. Each surrogate receives the same gap positions and the same linear interpolation as the data. We report $p = P(\tau^{*} \ge \tau)$, where $\tau^{*}$ is the surrogate statistic, with the correction $(k+1)/(N+1)$ for $k$ exceedances in $N$ surrogates; this is the tail in the direction of critical slowing down. The size of the procedure was checked on synthetic AR(1) series with no trend (600 series of 30 years, three interpolated points, coefficient 0.5), with the acceptance range of \SizeLo--\SizeHi\,\% for a nominal 5\,\% fixed beforehand. Power was examined in an earlier simulation, before any stellar series was analyzed, in which the AR(1) coefficient either stayed at 0.5 or rose linearly from 0.3 to 0.7 (600 series per length, window 50\,\%, no cycle); the quantity reported is the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of $\tau$ for trend against null. Because the surrogate model may absorb a cycle, a later simulation applied the whole surrogate test to series of 30 years with and without an added sinusoidal cycle (period \PCPeriod{} years, standard deviation about equal to that of the noise; \PCNRep{} series per case), with the same constant or rising coefficient, and reports the rejection rate at a nominal 5\,\%.

\subsection{State comparison for HD~166620}

For H1 we use the raw (not annual) series of Luhn et al.\ \cite{luhn2022}, in the three states they define: minimum~1 (1971--1975), minimum~2 (1987--1991) and the grand minimum (from 2004). The variability $\sigma_S$ of a state is the standard deviation of the interval with the instrumental noise variance removed in quadrature, the noise being \NoiseMW\,\% of the mean S for Mount Wilson and \NoiseKeck\,\% for Keck as quoted by Luhn et al.\ (method A, primary). As a sensitivity check, method B takes the mean over seasons of the same corrected value. Variances are compared with a two-sided $F$ test, using the raw values and the corrected values of methods A and B; the test treats observations as independent, which they are not (Sect.~\ref{sec:disc}).

\subsection{R$'_{\rm HK}$}

S is also converted to $R'_{\rm HK}$ with the recipe and color correction of Noyes et al.\ \cite{noyes1984}, using $B\!-\!V$ from the California Legacy Survey V catalog \cite{isaacson2024} when the star is in it and from Baliunas et al.\ otherwise. The photospheric term of that calibration is valid for $0.44 < B\!-\!V < 0.82$; stars outside are marked in Table~\ref{tab:seg}. For a given $B\!-\!V$ the conversion is linear in S, so $\tau$ is unchanged and the run serves as a check.

\subsection{Group comparisons}

Groups are compared per star with the lag-1 autocorrelation and the normalized variance (variance divided by the squared mean S) of the longest segment. The lag-1 autocorrelation is compared in all four configurations and the normalized variance in two (original series and $\sigma = 1$ residual). The primary pair is Var and Long together against Flat, lag-1 autocorrelation of the $\sigma = 1$ residual, one-sided Mann--Whitney test, fixed before the tests were read. The remaining pairs are five per block (Var, Long and Cycle against Flat, Var and Long against Cycle), in six blocks, hence 30 tests; they are corrected with the Holm procedure within each metric and configuration block, and Holm over all 30 tests is also reported (see below). The groups have four to eight stars, so the smallest attainable one-sided $p$ is \MinPCycleFlat{} (eight against four) or \MinPFourFour{} (four against four).

\subsection{Analysis log}\label{sec:log}

Fixed before the stellar run: the sample and groups, the segment rule, windows, detrending, surrogate design, H1 and H2, and the size check with its acceptance range. Decided after the run, without changing any computed value: (i) the tail of $p$ (upper tail; the lower tail is also computed); (ii) keeping the points flagged both as one-day and outlier. This affects two stars, HD~190360 and HD~217014; with the opposite choice HD~190360 would fall below the minimum length. (iii) The family of the Holm correction (blocks of five instead of all 30 tests). Both Holm families are reported. After the first stellar run, an independent review noted that the conversion from S to $R'_{\rm HK}$ is an affine change and should not alter the ARMA fit. It did: fitted on the raw scale of the series, the order chosen by the AIC depended on that scale. We changed the fit to the standardized series and repeated the stellar run once with the same seeds. The values of $\tau$ were unchanged; the surrogate $p$ values changed by up to \RunPMax{} (by more than 0.05 in \RunPShare\,\% of the cells), and the \RunFlagBoth{} cells flagged at a window of 50\,\% were the same in both runs. Only the second run is reported; the first is kept in the repository for this comparison. The power simulation with a cycle and the robustness checks (coverage, interpolation, white-noise reference) were added after the second run and did not change any computed value.

\subsection{Software}

Python with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, statsmodels and Matplotlib; the pipeline, the seeds (base \SeedBase) and the official run (the second, see Sect.~\ref{sec:log}) are in the repository.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Size and power of the surrogate test}

On synthetic AR(1) series with no trend, the test rejected at the 5\,\% level in \AutoUp\,\% of the series with the upper-tail $p$ and in \AutoLo\,\% with the lower-tail $p$, inside the accepted range; it is slightly conservative. In the earlier power simulation, the AUC of $\tau$ for a rising coefficient against a constant one was \AUCTwentyFour{} for segments of 24 years, \AUCThirty{} for 30 and \AUCForty{} for 40 (1 would be perfect discrimination, 0.5 none). At the lengths used here the indicator therefore separates a rising autocorrelation from a stationary one only weakly, even without a cycle in the series. With a cycle added, the test did not react to the rising coefficient: it rejected in \PCTrendCyc\,\% of the rising series and \PCNullCyc\,\% of the constant ones, against \PCTrendNo\,\% and \PCNullNo\,\% without the cycle (\PCNRep{} series per case, so each rate has a standard error of about \PCSE{} percentage points; one period and one amplitude only).

\subsection{H1: variability of HD~166620 by state}

Table~\ref{tab:h1} gives the three states. The mean S is lower in the grand minimum (\HOneMeanGrand) than in minimum~1 (\HOneMeanMinOne), a difference we did not test. The variability $\sigma_S$ (method A) falls from \HOneSigAMinOne{} in minimum~1 to \HOneSigAGrand{} in the grand minimum; minimum~2 has the largest value, \HOneSigAMinTwo{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:h1}). Method B gives the same ordering (\HOneSigBMinOne, \HOneSigBMinTwo, \HOneSigBGrand). The $F$ test of minimum~1 against the grand minimum gives $p = $ \HOnePRaw{} with the raw variances and $p = $ \HOnePCorrA{} with the corrected ones (method A), and $p = $ \HOnePCorrB{} with method B; minimum~2 against the grand minimum gives $p$~\HOnePTwoBound{} in every version. Method B gives a smaller corrected $\sigma_S$ in the grand minimum, hence the smaller $p$; the corrected values depend on the noise correction. The $p$ values are reported to one significant figure because the test treats clustered, autocorrelated observations as independent.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{State comparison} H1 asked whether the S-index variability of HD~166620 in the grand minimum is smaller than in the cycle minima. It is, by both methods and for both minima (Table~\ref{tab:h1}). We treat this as descriptive. It rests on one star, on single intervals, and on an $F$ test whose $p$ values are not credible at face value: the observations inside a season are strongly correlated (minimum~2 has \HOneNMinTwo{} points in four seasons), and the correction subtracts a fixed fraction of the mean as instrumental noise, with a different fraction for the instrument that observed the grand minimum. A lower variance in a lower-activity state is also what one expects without any dynamical transition, but the mean level does not explain much here: the mean S of the grand minimum is only \HOneMeanPct\,\% below that of minimum~2, while $\sigma_S$ falls by a factor of \HOneRatioA{} (method A; \HOneRatioRaw{} for the raw interval). The direction of the difference is the finding; its significance is not.

\paragraph{Temporal indicators} H2 was not supported: for HD~166620 the variance has $\tau \le 0$ for the $\sigma = 1$ residual and for the larger smoothing widths, and the lag-1 autocorrelation has $p \ge 0.05$ in all four configurations. But the test was not possible as designed. The Mount Wilson record of HD~166620 ends in \HDHEnd, before the transition, and we could not find the original Mount Wilson observations for 1995--2002 in a public source, so the approach to the grand minimum is not sampled. The other \NOtherSeg{} segments are series with no known transition; they form a null scan, and the absence of a flagged segment is the expected outcome when nothing is there to find. A decisive test needs an annual or finer series that runs through a transition.

\paragraph{Power} The surrogate test has nominal size, slightly conservative (\AutoUp\,\% rejection of null series with the upper-tail $p$ at a nominal 5\,\%), and in \NCellsHalf{} combinations it flagged \NSigHalf{} where about \NChance{} would be expected by chance at the nominal 5\,\% (about \NChanceEff{} at the size measured), if the combinations were independent, which they are not. Its power is low: AUC of \AUCTwentyFour{} to \AUCForty{} for a rising autocorrelation without any cycle, at the simulated lengths of \PowLMin{} to \PowLMax{} years (the Sun, with \NSunYears{} years, lies outside that range). With a deterministic cycle present, an ARMA model fitted to the series can absorb part of it, so the surrogate null mimics the cycle; in our simulation the test then did not react at all to a rising coefficient (rejection \PCTrendCyc\,\% against \PCNullCyc\,\% for the constant one). That simulation used a single period and amplitude, and a curve over amplitude and period at the real lengths was not computed. A null outcome here is therefore weak evidence against critical slowing down before a loss of cycle.

\paragraph{What the indicators measure} A Gaussian smooth of width one year keeps most of a cycle of \PerTen{} to \PerSixteen{} years, so the $\sigma = 1$ residual keeps only \ResAmpTen, \ResAmpTwelve{} and \ResAmpSixteen\,\% of the amplitude of cycles of \PerTen, \PerTwelve{} and \PerSixteen{} years. The negative median lag-1 autocorrelation of the residual (\MedACSOne, against \MedACNone{} for the original series) is mostly the filter: white noise passed through the same filter would have \WNACOne{} (\WNACThree{} and \WNACFive{} for widths of 3 and 5 years). The observed median lies above that value, so the residual keeps some positive autocorrelation beyond white noise, as a cycle or other slow variation would leave; we did not show which. Windows of \WinMin{} to \WinMax{} points (50\,\%) cover about one cycle, so the rolling indicators follow cycle phase more than recovery rate, and the four configurations are not four independent views of the noise. The normalized variance differs between Cycle and Flat stars without any critical slowing down, through the cycle amplitude alone. We attribute the two comparisons that survive within-block Holm correction to this, as an interpretation we did not test; they do not survive Holm over all tests.

\paragraph{Limits} Segments with interpolated years (up to ten in one star), stars outside the validity range of the $R'_{\rm HK}$ calibration, and the composite character of the solar series are marked in Table~\ref{tab:seg}; in a check from the results, \NInterpFlagUp{} of the \NSigHalf{} cells flagged at a window of 50\,\% lies among the \NInterpCellsUp{} cells of segments with at most two interpolated years, and the other lies in HD~\SigTwoHD, one of \NInterpOver{} segments with more than two (the surrogates receive the same gaps and interpolation as the data, but we did not measure the effect of the interpolation on the indicators). Annual means rest on few observations in some years: the median number of observations per year in a segment is \CovMed{} at the median of the \NCovSeg{} star segments, but \NCovLow{} of them have at least one year with fewer than five observations (minimum \CovMin). The Sun is the only segment labeled adequate, but its level can shift where instruments change. Group sizes (four to eight) make every group test low in power, and stars were included according to the availability of a series, so the groups are convenience samples. The next steps are a repeat on a series that crosses a known transition, a positive control on a dynamical model with a slowly changing parameter, and a power curve over cycle period and amplitude at the real segment lengths.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The S-index variability of HD~166620 in its grand minimum is lower than in both earlier cycle minima. This is descriptive, from one star and published intervals.
\item The rolling lag-1 autocorrelation and variance of annual Mount Wilson series, in \NSegments{} segments of \LMin{} to \NSunYears{} years, show no consistent rise. For HD~166620 the test could not be made as designed because its record ends before the transition, and the test has little power against the modest rise simulated (AUC \AUCTwentyFour{} to \AUCForty{}) and none in the simulation with a cycle. Nothing follows about stars that are about to lose a cycle.
\item A decisive test needs a series that runs through a transition, a positive control on a dynamical model, a power curve over cycle period and amplitude, and an analysis of the loss of a limit cycle, for which we know of no predicted sign.
\end{enumerate}
